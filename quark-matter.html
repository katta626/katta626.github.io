Quark matter in neutron stars

Could the core of a neutron star melt into free quarks, and what would that change about the stars we can measure?

Inside a neutron star

A neutron star is what remains when the core of a massive star collapses. Gravity is held off by the pressure of degenerate neutrons and by the strong force between them. Going inward, the density rises from that of ordinary iron to several times the density inside an atomic nucleus.

The equation of state

Everything about the star follows from how pressure P depends on energy density ε. Given that relation, the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equations of general relativity fix the mass and radius of every possible star.

The trouble is the inner core. Laboratory nuclei reach only about n0, and quantum chromodynamics cannot yet be solved at the densities beyond. So the equation of state there is written flexibly and tested against the stars themselves through their mass and radius.

dPdr=− (ε+P)(m+4πr3P) r2(1−2m/r) dmdr=4πr2ε

From hadrons to quarks

At high enough density, neutrons may overlap until their quarks are no longer confined. The Maxwell construction treats this as a sharp boundary at constant pressure. Finite-size effects can instead let the two phases coexist: if surface tension is low the mixed phase is extended, as in the Gibbs construction, with droplets, rods, slabs and bubbles known as pasta. If it is high, the behaviour is close to Maxwell.

I described hadronic and quark matter with agnostic piecewise polytropes, and joined them with a quadratic mixed phase that passes through Pc + ΔP, with pressure and density matching at both ends. ΔP = 0 recovers the Maxwell construction. Raising it widens the mixed phase and smooths the transition.

PM(μ)=α(μ−μc)2+β(μ−μc)+Pc+ΔP

α, β and the two ends of the mixed phase, μcH and μcQ, follow from the boundary conditions.

Mass, radius and twin stars

A strong first-order transition can produce a third family of stars, and with it twin stars: the same mass, different radii. As ΔP rises the maximum mass rises and the third-family branch shrinks and vanishes.

There is also a largest ΔP an equation of state can carry. It falls as the quark matter stiffens (higher ΓQ) and rises almost linearly with the critical chemical potential μcrit. The ΔP at which twins disappear follows the same two trends.
