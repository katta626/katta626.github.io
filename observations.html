Are magnetars responsible for FRBs?

Magnetars carry the strongest magnetic fields known, around 1014 to 1015 gauss, and spin slowly, once every few seconds. As the field twists and breaks, the crust cracks and the star flares in X-rays and γ-rays.

Fast radio bursts are millisecond flashes, usually from other galaxies, and their source has been an open question. In April 2020 the Galactic magnetar SGR J1935+2154 produced FRB 200428, the first fast radio burst traced to a known source. It tied at least some of these bursts to magnetars.

I run targeted low-frequency observations of this magnetar and analyse them for radio emission linked to its activity.

Emission profiles of PSR J1921+2153

A pulsar’s magnetic axis is tilted from its spin axis. Charged particles stream out along the open field lines above each magnetic pole and emit a narrow beam of coherent radio waves. Each turn the beam sweeps past Earth once, and we see a pulse.

Averaged over thousands of turns the pulse looks steady; one turn at a time it is not. Bright subpulses can march across the pulse window from turn to turn (subpulse drifting), and flares can return at the same rotational phase (phase-locked intermittent brightness). Both map how the plasma above the pole is organised.

I study these single pulses in more than 300 days of observations at 175 MHz.

A note on spin and age

The star on the first page spins for display, not to scale. Real neutron stars turn anywhere from hundreds of times a second to once every several seconds. Pulsars like the one I observe are millions of years old; magnetars are young, thousands to tens of thousands of years, and slow.

The equation of state describes a cold, stable star in equilibrium. A neutron star cools into that state within its first years, and spin changes its structure only slightly, so the same physics of the core applies to an old pulsar and a young magnetar alike.
