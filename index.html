Radio emission from pulsars

A pulsar is a spinning neutron star whose magnetic axis is tilted from its spin axis. Charged particles stream out along the open field lines above each magnetic pole and emit a narrow beam of coherent radio waves. Each turn the beam sweeps past Earth once, and we see a pulse.

Averaged over thousands of turns the pulse looks steady; one turn at a time it is not. Bright subpulses can march across the pulse window from turn to turn (subpulse drifting), and flares can return at the same rotational phase (phase-locked intermittent brightness). Both map how the plasma above the pole is organised.

I study these single pulses in more than 300 days of observations at 175 MHz.

Magnetars and fast radio bursts

Magnetars carry the strongest magnetic fields known, around 1014 to 1015 gauss, and spin slowly, once every few seconds. As the field twists and breaks, the crust cracks and the star flares in X-rays and γ-rays.

In April 2020 the Galactic magnetar SGR J1935+2154 produced FRB 200428, the first fast radio burst traced to a known source. It tied at least some of these millisecond bursts to magnetars. I run targeted low-frequency observations of this magnetar and look for radio emission linked to its activity.

A note on spin and age

The star on this page spins for display, not to scale. Real neutron stars turn anywhere from hundreds of times a second to once every several seconds. Pulsars like the one I observe are millions of years old; magnetars are young, thousands to tens of thousands of years, and slow.

The equation of state below describes a cold, stable star in equilibrium. A neutron star cools into that state within its first years, and spin changes its structure only slightly, so the same physics of the core applies to an old pulsar and a young magnetar alike.

The signal travels: dispersion

On its way to us a radio pulse crosses thin ionised gas between the stars. Lower frequencies travel slower, so the pulse arrives as a sweep: high frequencies first, low ones later. The delay grows with the dispersion measure, DM, the column of free electrons along the line of sight.

Δt≈4.15 ms×DM× (flo−2−fhi−2)

DM in pc cm−3, frequencies in GHz.

Shift each channel back by its delay and sum: the pulse snaps into a sharp peak. Get the DM wrong and it smears into the noise. For the Diamond Array Radio Telescope (DART) I built the pipeline that does this for fast radio bursts: ADC samples to filterbank data, processed in parallel, with GPU interference removal and real-time detection under way.

Inside the star: hadrons to quarks

Under the crust, neutrons crowd closer with depth until, in the core, they may overlap so much that their quarks no longer belong to any one of them: hadronic matter becomes deconfined quark matter. The cutaway shows both: rings of three quarks are neutrons; loose quarks are quark matter.

The usual model, the Maxwell construction, makes the change a sharp boundary at one pressure. Surface tension and charge screening can instead let the two phases mix over a range of depth in droplets, rods and slabs, a hadron–quark pasta. In my thesis I modelled that mixed phase with one extra pressure, ΔP, added at the transition:

PM(μ)=α(μ−μc)2+β(μ−μc)+Pc+ΔP

Hadronic and quark phases as piecewise polytropes; the quadratic joins them with matching pressure and density at both ends.

What it changes: mass, radius and twin stars

Solving the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equations for each equation of state gives the family of stars it allows. A strong, sharp transition can break that family: stars just past the transition are unstable, and a second, more compact branch appears. Two stable stars then share one mass with different radii, twin stars. In my models twins appeared at 1.66 solar masses with radii of 10 and 12.25 km.

Raising ΔP smooths the transition, raises the maximum mass and, past a threshold, removes the twins. That threshold falls as the quark matter stiffens and rises as the transition moves deeper into the star. There is also a largest ΔP an equation of state can carry and stay thermodynamically consistent.
